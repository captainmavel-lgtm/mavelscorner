### And Jeremiah Sat Down In The Ruins Of Jerusalem.

The book of Lamentations was written over a city that no longer existed. Jerusalem had been besieged, breached, and emptied. The temple, which had stood as the dwelling place of God among His people, lay in ashes. The people Jeremiah had spent his entire ministry warning, pleading with, and weeping over had been bound and marched away to Babylon. Everything he had given his life to was gone, and he had watched all of it happen.

Most of the book reads the way you would expect a man in that position to write. He speaks of being driven into darkness rather than light, of his flesh and skin being made old, of being walled in with no way out and his prayer shut out along with him. He does not soften any of it. He does not reach for an explanation that would make the ruin easier to look at.

And then, in the middle of the third chapter, in the middle of the grief and without any change in his circumstances, he writes this:

> *Through the LORD’s mercies we are not consumed, because His compassions fail not. They are new every morning; great is Your faithfulness. Lamentations 3:22-23*

He was not standing on the far side of recovery when he said it. The rubble had not been cleared. Nothing had been rebuilt. He wrote the most confident statement about God’s faithfulness in all of Scripture while sitting inside the worst loss of his life.

### Mercy Written From The Rubble.

What makes Lamentations 3:22 remarkable is where Jeremiah places the credit. He does not say the people survived because they were resilient. He does not say he personally endured because his faith held. He says they were not consumed through the mercies of the LORD. The survival belongs entirely to God.

That single sentence quietly removes every other explanation. God’s mercy held them when every visible support had already collapsed, and His mercy is the only reason He ever survived to write that Book of the bible.

He follows it with a claim about supply. His compassions fail not. They do not thin out with heavy use. They do not grow cold after the tenth visit with the same struggle. Human compassion works differently, and anyone who has leaned hard on another person knows the point where even a generous friend reaches the end of what they have to give. God’s compassion has no such edge, because it flows from His nature rather than from a reserve that empties.

### The Morning Is the Proof.

Then comes the line most people know by heart without knowing where it was written. They are new every morning.

Jeremiah is not describing a sunrise he happened to enjoy. He is making a theological claim about how God relates to time. Yesterday’s mercy was spent on yesterday. It was not rationed to stretch into today. When the sun comes up over a life that has not improved at all since the night before, a fresh supply has already arrived with it.

Which means the morning after the hardest night of your life is not a morning you face uncovered. It is a morning carrying mercy that has never been used. The sunrise you did not feel strong enough to meet is itself the evidence that God renewed His commitment to carry you while you slept.

Notice also what Jeremiah does immediately before the famous verses. He writes in verse 21, "This I recall to my mind, therefore I have hope." He calls his mind back. Not to his circumstances, which were unbearable, and not to an explanation, which he never received. He deliberately turns his attention to who God is, and hope rises out of that act of remembering rather than out of any improvement in his situation.

### Faithful Is What He Is, Not What He Does.

Centuries later Paul writes to a church in Corinth that was, by every visible measure, a mess. They were divided over which leader to follow. They were tolerating open immorality. They were confused and competitive about spiritual gifts. If any congregation had given God reason to withdraw, it was this one.
Paul opens the letter by telling them that God is faithful, and that He called them into the fellowship of His Son, Jesus Christ our Lord.

Read the grammar carefully, because the whole weight of the verse sits in it. Paul does not write that God acts faithfully, which would make faithfulness a behaviour God chooses to perform when the circumstances suit Him. He writes that God is faithful. Faithfulness is a property of His being. He can no more be unfaithful than He can lie or cease to exist.

That changes what your fear is actually about. When you worry that God might finally let you down, you are not bracing for something inside the range of His possibilities. You are bracing for something He is incapable of. His faithfulness was never conditional on your consistency, your performance, or the quality of your faith on a given day. It proceeds from who He is, which is why it holds steady on the days you bring Him nothing at all.

### He Will Not Let You Fall Out Of His Hands.

The call into fellowship with Christ began as God’s initiative, and God’s faithfulness is what keeps it intact. That is why Paul could write to the Corinthians the way he did. The fractures in that church were real and he spends the rest of the letter confronting them, but the fractures did not undo the call. The mess did not erase the foundation underneath it.

And the verse just before it tells you how far that goes. Paul says God will also confirm you to the end, blameless in the day of our Lord Jesus Christ, and then gives the reason in verse 9: God is faithful. The work He began in you, the calling, the slow reshaping, the hope that keeps returning even after you have given up on it, is not a project He walked away from.

He does not start what He will not finish. The same faithfulness that brought you in is the faithfulness that will carry you all the way out the other side. You will not be found wanting on the last day, because He will have been faithful on every single day between this one and that one.

### The Mercy Already Waiting For Tomorrow.

If God is faithful in the way Lamentations and 1 Corinthians describe Him, then doubt is not the most honest response available to you when life goes hard. Recall is. The move Jeremiah made from inside the ashes is the same one offered to you every time your circumstances try to rewrite your understanding of God. Call it back to mind. Who He is. What He has already done. What He has promised and cannot unsay.

Begin tomorrow by acknowledging Him before you reach for your phone, because the mercy for that day will already have arrived ahead of you. Write down what He has done, so that the record you keep in a good season becomes the thing you read in a hard one. Refuse to let a question you cannot answer become your definition of a God you already know. And say it out loud, the way Jeremiah wrote it down and Paul declared it, because naming His faithfulness has a way of resizing everything standing in front of you.

He is not a God who almost came through. He is not a God who tried and got close. His compassions held you through last night, they will meet you again in the morning, and He is far too faithful to fail you now.
